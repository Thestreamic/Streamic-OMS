This is where connected media workflows come into play, aiming to provide a unified and integrated platform for media operations. For broadcast teams, the interesting question is how to leverage connected media workflows to improve their operations and deliver high-quality content across multiple platforms. However, it also requires careful planning, integration, and testing to ensure seamless operation and minimal disruption to existing workflows.

Under the Hood: How It Actually Works

In terms of technical implementation, connected media workflows are likely to leverage industry-standard protocols and technologies, such as SMPTE ST 2110, AES67, and IP core, to ensure seamless integration with existing infrastructure and systems. This can enable broadcasters to scale their operations more efficiently, while also improving their ability to deliver high-quality content across multiple platforms. For example, a connected media workflow might involve ingest of live feeds via ST 2110, followed by processing and tagging using AI-powered tools, and then distribution to multiple platforms via IP-based infrastructure. The use of NMOS discovery and control protocols can facilitate the discovery and registration of devices on the network, making it easier to manage and monitor the workflow. A typical system flow might involve live feed ingestion via ST 2110 → processing and tagging using Avid MediaCentral or Adobe Premiere Pro → distribution to multiple platforms via IP-based infrastructure and CDN → delivery to viewers via HLS, DASH, or CMAF. This changes the calculus for broadcast teams, as they can now focus on creating high-quality content rather than worrying about the underlying infrastructure. The use of PTP/IEEE 1588 timing and synchronization protocols can ensure that all devices on the network are synchronized, reducing the risk of errors and ensuring seamless operation. Additionally, the use of hybrid SDI/IP design can facilitate a smooth transition from traditional SDI-based infrastructure to IP-based infrastructure. The use of multivendor interoperability protocols, such as NMOS, can facilitate the integration of devices from different vendors, making it easier to build and manage connected media workflows. For example, a broadcaster might use a combination of EVS replay systems, Vizrt graphics, and Avid Media Composer editing systems, all integrated via NMOS and ST 2110. This can enable broadcasters to create complex and sophisticated workflows, while also reducing the risk of errors and improving overall efficiency. In practice, this means that broadcast teams can focus on creating high-quality content, rather than worrying about the underlying infrastructure and technical details.

What This Looks Like in a Real Broadcast Operation

In a real broadcast operation, connected media workflows might be used to streamline the ingest-to-playout chain, reducing the time and effort required to deliver content to multiple platforms. For example, a newsroom might use Avid MediaCentral to ingest and manage live feeds, and then use Vizrt graphics to add overlays and branding. The resulting content could then be distributed to multiple platforms via IP-based infrastructure and CDN, using protocols such as HLS, DASH, or CMAF. This can enable broadcasters to deliver high-quality content quickly and efficiently, while also reducing the risk of errors and improving overall efficiency. In a live production environment, connected media workflows might be used to facilitate the integration of multiple systems and devices, such as EVS replay systems, Ross video switchers, and Chyron graphics. This can enable broadcasters to create complex and sophisticated live productions, while also reducing the risk of errors and improving overall efficiency. For example, a live production might involve the use of multiple cameras, each feeding into a Ross video switcher, which then feeds into an EVS replay system for highlighting and analysis. The resulting content could then be distributed to multiple platforms via IP-based infrastructure and CDN, using protocols such as HLS, DASH, or CMAF.

The Practical Impact: Cost, Speed, and Scale

The practical impact of connected media workflows is significant, as they can help broadcasters reduce operational costs, improve efficiency, and enhance content delivery capabilities. By automating repetitive tasks and streamlining workflows, connected media workflows can reduce the time and effort required to deliver content to multiple platforms. This can enable broadcasters to focus on creating high-quality content, rather than worrying about the underlying infrastructure and technical details. Additionally, connected media workflows can facilitate the integration of multiple systems and devices, reducing the risk of errors and improving overall efficiency. In terms of cost, connected media workflows can help broadcasters reduce their infrastructure and operational costs, by facilitating the use of IP-based infrastructure and cloud-based services. This can enable broadcasters to scale their operations more efficiently, while also reducing their capital and operational expenditures. For example, a broadcaster might use AWS Media Services or Azure to host their connected media workflow, reducing the need for on-premises infrastructure and improving scalability. Additionally, connected media workflows can facilitate the use of software-defined workflows, reducing the need for proprietary hardware and improving flexibility.

The Reality Check

However, it's also important to consider the potential challenges and limitations of connected media workflows. Additionally, the use of IP-based infrastructure and cloud-based services can introduce new security risks and operational complexities, such as latency and packet loss.
