Utah Scientific To Spotlight Hybrid SDI-IP Portfolio At 2026 NAB Show

Utah Scientific To Spotlight Hybrid SDI-IP Portfolio At 2026 NAB Show

The Streamic Editorial Team March 23, 2026 665 words · 3 min read Analysis
Source Attribution This analysis is based on publicly available reporting from: TV Technology (March 2026).
This article provides independent technical interpretation by The Streamic.

The Quick Hit

Utah Scientific is bringing a new lineup of gear to NAB 2026 that lets broadcasters mix old-school SDI cables with modern IP networks—cutting latency and keeping signals rock-solid without ripping out existing infrastructure.

What Happened

  • Utah Scientific will showcase its hybrid SDI-IP portfolio at the 2026 NAB Show, designed to bridge traditional SDI infrastructure with IP-based signal transport [Source 1].
  • The portfolio supports key broadcast standards like SMPTE ST 2110 and NMOS, enabling live signal transport with lower latency and improved signal integrity compared to satellite or fiber-based methods [Source 1].
  • Products in the portfolio are built to integrate with existing broadcast IT stacks, including support for AES67 audio-over-IP and JPEG XS compression for efficient video transport [Source 1].

The Tech Spec

Utah Scientific’s hybrid SDI-IP portfolio is essentially a translator between two worlds. Think of it like a universal power adapter: on one side, you plug in your legacy SDI cables (the ones that have been carrying video signals for decades), and on the other side, you connect to an IP network (like swapping a landline for Wi-Fi, but without losing call quality). This setup lets broadcasters keep their existing cameras, switchers, and replay servers while gradually shifting to IP-based workflows. The portfolio leans on SMPTE ST 2110 (a set of rules for sending video, audio, and metadata as separate streams over IP, like splitting a movie into its soundtrack, subtitles, and visuals so they can travel independently and sync up at the destination) and NMOS (a control system for IP devices, like a traffic cop directing which signals go where in a network). For audio, it supports AES67 (a standard for sending high-quality audio over IP, like upgrading from a cassette tape to streaming music), ensuring compatibility with other IP-based audio gear. The portfolio also uses JPEG XS compression (a lightweight way to squeeze video into smaller files without noticeable quality loss, like zipping a photo before emailing it) to keep bandwidth usage efficient. Under the hood, the system requires a 10GbE network (a fast network backbone, like upgrading from a garden hose to a firehose for data) to handle the IP traffic. It’s designed to work with hardware and software from other major broadcast vendors, so engineers won’t need to overhaul their entire setup to adopt it [Source 1].

So What? (The Human Impact)

For broadcast engineers and operations teams, this portfolio could mean fewer headaches during live productions. The hybrid approach reduces latency—cutting delays by as little as 10ms—which matters for live sports or breaking news where every millisecond counts [Source 1]. It also simplifies signal routing, making it easier to manage complex setups (imagine fewer cables to trace and fewer points of failure). The shift to IP can shrink hardware footprints, freeing up rack space and potentially lowering power and cooling costs. For CTOs, the appeal is twofold: cost savings and future-proofing. IP-based transport can be cheaper than satellite or fiber, and the hybrid model lets broadcasters migrate at their own pace without stranding existing SDI investments. The support for standards like ST 2110 and NMOS also means less risk of vendor lock-in, giving teams more flexibility to mix and match gear as needs evolve [Source 1].

  • The source doesn’t detail pricing or licensing models (e.g., one-time purchase, subscription, or pay-per-use).
  • While the portfolio is said to integrate with “major broadcast equipment manufacturers,” the source doesn’t name which vendors or products are officially supported.
  • The source mentions “reduced transmission costs” but doesn’t quantify how much cheaper IP transport is compared to satellite or fiber in real-world scenarios.
  • The source doesn’t clarify whether the portfolio includes built-in redundancy or failover mechanisms for critical live productions.
