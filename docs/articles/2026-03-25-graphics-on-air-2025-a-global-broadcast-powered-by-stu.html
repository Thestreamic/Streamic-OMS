On Air 2025: A Global Broadcast Powered by Students and Vizrt

On Air 2025: A Global Broadcast Powered by Students and Vizrt

The Streamic Editorial Team March 25, 2026 853 words · 4 min read

Vizrt's "On Air 2025" Signals a Shift Towards IP-Native, Distributed Graphics Workflows

The push for globally distributed, IP-based live production is accelerating, driven by the need for greater flexibility and reduced operational overhead. This trend, exemplified by Vizrt's "On Air 2025" initiative, signifies a move away from centralized, hardware-bound graphics operations towards a more fluid, software-defined model. It’s about democratizing access to high-end graphics capabilities, enabling smaller teams or even student productions to achieve broadcast-quality results without the massive infrastructure investment previously required. This architectural evolution is directly impacting how live content is conceived, produced, and delivered, particularly for organizations seeking to scale their operations rapidly or engage remote talent.

Technical Deep Dive

The core of this shift lies in leveraging IP transport protocols like ST 2110 and NDI for signal distribution, alongside cloud-native graphics engines. Vizrt's approach likely involves their Viz Engine, a powerful real-time graphics renderer, being accessible over IP networks. This allows for graphics operators to be located anywhere, controlling instances of Viz Engine deployed in various locations – be it on-premise, in a colocation facility, or even within a cloud provider's infrastructure. The use of ST 2110-21 for uncompressed video and AES67 for audio ensures interoperability within a modern IP broadcast plant, while NDI offers a more flexible, lower-bandwidth alternative for less demanding applications or for connecting disparate systems. This distributed model bypasses the need for extensive SDI cabling and complex routing matrices, relying instead on robust IP networking and potentially protocols like NMOS for discovery and control. The dependency shifts from physical signal paths to network fabric stability and IP address management.

This contrasts sharply with traditional broadcast graphics workflows that relied on dedicated SDI connections from graphics hardware to switchers and routers. The architectural change is profound: instead of a graphics machine being physically tethered to a specific studio or control room, it becomes a network-addressable resource. This removes the physical dependency and introduces a reliance on IP network infrastructure and software-based control planes.

Why This Matters in 2026

For regional broadcasters and smaller production houses, this means a significant reduction in CapEx. Instead of purchasing dedicated graphics hardware for each studio or control room, they can invest in a pool of IP-addressable graphics resources that can be dynamically allocated. This translates to lower transmission costs for remote productions, as high-quality IP streams can be used instead of expensive satellite or dedicated fibre links. The operational benefit is a simplified signal flow; graphics can be "patched" to any output via software, reducing the need for manual re-cabling and the associated engineering headcount for complex live events. A regional sports broadcaster, for instance, could use a single instance of a high-end graphics engine for multiple games happening simultaneously across different venues, simply by routing the NDI or ST 2110 streams to the appropriate instance.

  • For broadcast engineers: This necessitates a deep understanding of IP networking, including multicast, IGMP snooping, and QoS configurations. Integrating Vizrt's IP-enabled graphics with existing ST 2110 infrastructure will require careful planning around NMOS IS-04/IS-05 for device registration and connection management, ensuring graphics sources are discoverable and routable.
  • For OTT and streaming platforms: While not directly impacting ABR encoding, the ability to generate sophisticated graphics remotely and feed them into an IP-based contribution chain can significantly enhance the perceived quality of live streams. This could lead to more dynamic ad insertion opportunities or richer on-screen graphics for niche streaming services.
  • For MAM and post-production teams: The impact is less direct but could involve integrating graphics templates and assets into a centralized MAM for easier access by remote operators. Metadata associated with graphics elements might need to be standardized for better searchability and recall across distributed production environments.

Limitations & Engineering Risks

  • Latency and timing constraints: While IP protocols like ST 2110 offer low latency, the overall glass-to-glass latency will depend on the entire signal chain, including network hops, encoding/decoding, and the graphics rendering time. PTP (Precision Time Protocol) jitter must be meticulously managed to ensure frame-accurate synchronization, especially if graphics are being keyed over live video feeds. A typical target for live production is sub-frame latency for critical signals.
  • Vendor lock-in exposure: While ST 2110 and AES67 are open standards, the integration of Vizrt's graphics engines and control software may introduce proprietary dependencies. The specific APIs and control protocols used for dynamic allocation and configuration could create switching costs if a broadcaster wishes to adopt a different graphics vendor in the future.
  • Integration complexity: Ensuring interoperability between Vizrt's IP graphics and other ST 2110 devices (e.g., Grass Valley switchers, EVS replay servers) requires careful adherence to standards. Issues can arise with NMOS implementation differences, where a graphics source might not be correctly registered or discoverable by a central control system, leading to connection failures.
  • Skills and operational gap: Broadcast engineering teams traditionally focused on SDI infrastructure may lack the deep expertise in IP networking, firewall configurations, and software-defined control required for these distributed IP workflows. Understanding network segmentation, VLANs, and multicast routing is now as critical as understanding a video router.
