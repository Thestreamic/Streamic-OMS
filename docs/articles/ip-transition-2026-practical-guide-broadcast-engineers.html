The IP Transition in 2026: A Practical Engineering Guide for Broadcasters

The transition from traditional **SDI (Serial Digital Interface)** to **IP (Internet Protocol)**-based infrastructure is a complex process that poses significant technical and operational challenges for broadcasters. As the industry continues to evolve, small and mid-market broadcasters are under increasing pressure to adapt to the new **SMPTE ST 2110** standard, which promises to bring numerous benefits, including greater flexibility, scalability, and cost savings. In this article, we will delve into the real-world challenges and solutions for broadcasters migrating to **IP** infrastructure, with a focus on practical engineering guidance for small and mid-market broadcasters.

Understanding the Challenges of IP Transition

One of the primary challenges facing broadcasters is the need to balance the benefits of **IP** infrastructure with the complexities of implementation.

The IP transition is not just a technical challenge, but also a cultural and operational one. It requires a fundamental shift in the way we think about broadcast infrastructure and the way we work – Mike Cronk, Senior Vice President of Core Technology at Grass Valley
. As broadcasters navigate this transition, they must consider factors such as **network architecture**, **traffic management**, and **quality of service (QoS)**, all of which are critical to ensuring a smooth and reliable broadcast experience.

SMPTE ST 2110 and NMOS: The Foundations of IP Infrastructure

The **SMPTE ST 2110** standard provides a common framework for **IP**-based broadcast infrastructure, enabling the transport of **video**, **audio**, and **ancillary data** over **IP** networks. **NMOS (Networked Media Open Specification)**, on the other hand, provides a set of **APIs (Application Programming Interfaces)** and **data models** that enable the discovery, registration, and control of **IP**-based devices. Together, these standards provide a solid foundation for building scalable and flexible **IP** infrastructure. Broadcasters can use **SMPTE ST 2110** to transport **video** and **audio** essence, while **NMOS** enables the discovery and registration of devices, making it easier to manage complex **IP** networks.

Hybrid Approaches: A Practical Solution for Small and Mid-Market Broadcasters

While **SMPTE ST 2110** and **NMOS** provide a solid foundation for **IP** infrastructure, the reality is that many small and mid-market broadcasters may not have the resources or budget to implement a fully **IP**-based system. In these cases, **hybrid approaches** can provide a practical solution, allowing broadcasters to leverage the benefits of **IP** infrastructure while still utilizing existing **SDI** equipment. By using **gateways** and **converters**, broadcasters can bridge the gap between **SDI** and **IP**, enabling them to transition to **IP** infrastructure at their own pace.

Audio Over IP: The Role of AES67 and Other Standards

When it comes to **audio over IP**, broadcasters have a range of options, including **AES67**, **Dante**, and **Ravenna**. **AES67**, in particular, provides a common framework for **audio over IP**, enabling the transport of **audio** essence over **IP** networks. By using **AES67**, broadcasters can ensure interoperability between different **audio** devices and systems, making it easier to manage complex **audio** workflows. Other standards, such as **SRT (Secure Reliable Transport)** and **RIST (Reliable Internet Stream Transport)**, provide additional options for **audio** and **video** transport over **IP** networks.

Video Over IP: The Role of HEVC, AV1, and Other Codecs

When it comes to **video over IP**, broadcasters have a range of options, including **HEVC (High Efficiency Video Coding)**, **AV1 (AOMedia Video 1)**, and **H.264 (MPEG-4 AVC)**. **HEVC**, in particular, provides a highly efficient **video** codec, enabling the transport of high-quality **video** over **IP** networks. **AV1**, on the other hand, provides an open-source alternative to **HEVC**, offering similar levels of efficiency and quality. By using these **codecs**, broadcasters can ensure high-quality **video** transport over **IP** networks, while also reducing bandwidth requirements.

Real-World Implementations: Case Studies and Best Practices

So, how are broadcasters implementing **IP** infrastructure in the real world? A recent case study by **Imagine Communications** highlights the use of **SMPTE ST 2110** and **NMOS** to build a scalable and flexible **IP**-based broadcast system. The system, which was implemented for a major broadcaster, enables the transport of **video**, **audio**, and **ancillary data** over **IP** networks, using **SMPTE ST 2110** and **NMOS** to ensure interoperability and discoverability. Other case studies, such as the use of **NDI (Network Device Interface)** for **IP**-based live production, demonstrate the flexibility and scalability of **IP** infrastructure in real-world broadcast applications.

Best Practices for IP Infrastructure Implementation

So, what are the best practices for implementing **IP** infrastructure? Here are a few key takeaways:

  • **Start small**: Begin with a small-scale **IP** infrastructure implementation and scale up as needed.
  • **Choose the right standards**: Select **SMPTE ST 2110**, **NMOS**, and other relevant standards to ensure interoperability and discoverability.
  • **Use hybrid approaches**: Leverage **gateways** and **converters** to bridge the gap between **SDI** and **IP** equipment.
  • **Monitor and analyze**: Use **monitoring** and **analytics** tools to ensure **QoS** and **network performance**.
By following these best practices, broadcasters can ensure a smooth and successful transition to **IP** infrastructure, while also minimizing downtime and ensuring high-quality broadcast experiences.

The Future of IP Infrastructure: Trends and Predictions

So, what does the future hold for **IP** infrastructure? As **5G** and other emerging technologies become more prevalent, we can expect to see increased demand for **IP**-based broadcast systems that can handle high-bandwidth, low-latency **video** and **audio** transport. The use of **cloud** and **edge computing** will also become more widespread, enabling broadcasters to leverage **IP** infrastructure to build more scalable and flexible broadcast systems. By 2030, we can expect **IP** infrastructure to be the norm, with **SDI** becoming increasingly obsolete. As the industry continues to evolve, it's essential for broadcasters to stay ahead of the curve, investing in **IP** infrastructure and developing the skills and expertise needed to succeed in an **IP**-based world.
