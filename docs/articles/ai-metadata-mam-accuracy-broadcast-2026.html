AI Metadata in the MAM: Why 95% Accuracy Is Still a Failing Grade

Every MAM vendor ships AI auto-tagging in 2026. The accuracy numbers describe benchmarks editors never hit. The gap between '95% correct' and 'useful for an editor on deadline' is where these systems still fail.

The Streamic Editorial Team March 18, 2026 502 words · 3 min read Analysis

Every major MAM and PAM vendor now includes AI-powered auto-tagging as a standard feature. The marketing numbers are consistent: 90–95% accuracy on metadata extraction. The operational reality is consistently different.

What 95% accuracy means in practice

A 95% accuracy rate on a metadata field means that 1 in 20 tags is wrong. For a library of 100,000 assets — a modest archive for a regional broadcaster — that means 5,000 incorrectly tagged items. Each incorrect tag creates a potential mismatch in search results, a compliance risk if the tag relates to rights or clearances, and an editorial risk if the tag affects content selection for a live programme.

The 95% figure also describes average accuracy across a benchmark dataset. Real archive content is not distributed like a benchmark dataset. Accuracy degrades significantly on archive footage with poor metadata provenance, on content shot in non-standard conditions, on faces and names that are not well-represented in the training data, and on domain-specific terminology that the model was not trained on.

Where the systems consistently fail

Speaker identification accuracy drops sharply for journalists, correspondents, and contributors who are well-known within an organisation but not prominent enough to appear frequently in public training data. A system that correctly identifies a major political figure 97% of the time may identify a regional political correspondent — who appears in thousands of clips — at 60–70% accuracy.

Temporal metadata — when exactly in a clip a specific event occurs — is consistently less accurate than presence/absence tagging. A system that correctly identifies that a clip contains a particular person or location may place that person or location at the wrong timestamp within the clip, causing editors to spend time locating the relevant moment that the AI was supposed to surface automatically.

The human review overhead that vendors omit

Most vendor case studies for AI metadata present the accuracy figure without quantifying the human review overhead required to bring that accuracy to an operational level. A system running at 95% accuracy on a high-volume ingest workflow still requires human review of every AI-generated tag before that tag is relied upon for rights clearance, compliance logging, or content selection for air.

The editorial time saved by not manually tagging content is partially offset by the editorial time required to review and correct AI-generated tags. For organisations with rigorous metadata standards — broadcast networks with compliance obligations, archives with rights management requirements — the net time saving is often smaller than the headline figure suggests.

What good looks like

The most effective AI metadata deployments in 2026 treat AI tagging as a drafting tool rather than a finalisation tool. AI generates candidate tags at ingest; human review confirms or corrects them before the tags are committed to the MAM record. The workflow is faster than manual tagging from scratch, and the final metadata quality is higher than unreviewed AI output. The business case should be built on this realistic workflow, not on the assumption that AI output can be used without review.
