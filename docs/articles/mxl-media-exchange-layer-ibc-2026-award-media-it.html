MXL Wins the IBC 2026 Content Creation Award: What the Media eXchange Layer Does, and Doesn't

MXL, the EBU's open-source Media eXchange Layer, won the IBC2026 Content Creation award. What it does, how mature it is, and what Media IT teams should do now.

The Streamic Editorial Team October 09, 2026 1,258 words · 6 min read Analysis

On the Sunday evening of IBC2026, the Media eXchange Layer (MXL) took the Content Creation category at the IBC Innovation Awards in Amsterdam. It is an open-source infrastructure project rather than a product, so it is worth being precise about what it is, what it is not, and how far along it really is.

1. What happened at IBC2026

MXL was announced as the Content Creation winner on 13 September 2026 at RAI Amsterdam. It is an open-source initiative started by the EBU together with the North American Broadcasters Association (NABA) and hosted by the Linux Foundation. TV Technology's winners list credits CBC/Radio-Canada with deploying it. The EBU's own write-up is more careful: it credits CBC/Radio-Canada with much of the day-to-day leadership and with co-chairing the project's Technical Steering Committee, but it gives no details of a production deployment. Treat "deployed" as a claim to ask about, not a fact to assume.

2. What MXL actually is

MXL is the media-exchange component of the EBU's Dynamic Media Facility (DMF) concept, which we covered in our DMF explainer. It lets software media functions pass uncompressed video, audio and timed data to each other through local and remote shared memory inside a processing cluster, instead of sending it over a network protocol.

Trade coverage describes the mechanics roughly like this: a "domain" is a namespace, typically a directory on a RAM-backed filesystem. A writer places a frame into a shared-memory region; readers map the same region read-only and use the pixels where they sit — no per-consumer copy. Across hosts, the design relies on RDMA-class networking. We have not inspected the SDK source ourselves, so verify those details against the project's repository before building on them.

3. What MXL is not

TechnologyMoves media between…Typical role
MXLSoftware functions sharing compute (one host or a tightly coupled cluster)Exchange layer inside a software-defined facility
SMPTE ST 2110Devices and facilities across an IP networkTransport between hardware and across the plant
SRT / RISTSites over the public internetContribution and ground-to-cloud transport

The EBU states plainly that MXL is not a replacement for ST 2110 between hardware devices, and vendor commentary adds that it is not designed for ground-to-cloud transport, which remains SRT and RIST territory. If a vendor pitch implies otherwise, push back.

4. Who is building it

  • Origin: EBU Members including CBC/Radio-Canada, BBC, France Télévisions, SR, SRG SSR, SVT and VRT identified media exchange as the key obstacle to multi-vendor interoperability after the DMF vision was presented at IBC 2024.
  • Technical Steering Committee co-chairs: CBC/Radio-Canada and Grass Valley.
  • Major code contributors: Grass Valley, Lawo and Riedel.
  • Requirements Council: chaired by the EBU and Lawo, meeting weekly. Members include AWS, Appear, BBC, NVIDIA, SWR, TVU Networks and Imagine Communications.
  • Wider community: names include Sony, Sky, Ross Video, Telestream, TAG Video Systems and Warner Bros. Discovery.

That breadth is the real story. Direct competitors are writing to the same exchange layer, which is exactly the multi-vendor interoperability problem the EBU members set out to solve.

5. How mature is it? An honest read

Award recognition is not production readiness. The most detailed maturity assessment we found comes from a vendor, Appear, so read it as one supplier's view:

  • Timeline: Linux Foundation project in April 2025, first alpha SDK in June 2025, public 1.0 SDK in December 2025, and a stable 1.0.0 specification and reference implementation in early 2026 (sources differ on whether that was late February or March).
  • Commercial availability: Appear cites a January 2026 SVG Europe survey that found no major broadcast vendor shipping a fully MXL-native commercial product. Existing work falls into lab prototypes, internal hybrid implementations behind conventional interfaces, and alignment work. Its forecast is partial commercialisation through the rest of 2026 and full multi-vendor production clusters in 2027 or later.
  • Known gaps: the specification does not define orchestration or workflow control, and it does not define wide-area media movement. BBC R&D's May 2026 tests of RDMA over OpenStack were described as promising, and an April 2026 AWS post said wide-area connectivity, plug-and-play device discovery and content authenticity still need to be built around MXL.

6. The Reuters connection — and a naming trap

The Content Distribution winner was also a "shared memory" story. Reuters replaced fixed-capacity infrastructure with a Global Shared Memory ecosystem powered by TVU Networks' MediaMesh, supporting event-driven, API-orchestrated news distribution, with partners including InSync, Kinetiq, Sony, Elecard and TAG Video Systems. Coverage based on TVU material says the architecture combines TAMS, EBU DMF, MXL and DPP LPX, and that when an event moves from preparation to live, MediaMesh activates the services it needs. Those descriptions come largely from the vendor, so the performance claims are unverified.

Our analysis: the phrase "shared memory" means two different things here. In MXL it is literal memory on a host or cluster. In TVU's usage it describes a wide-area layer that makes live signals reachable from anywhere. They look complementary rather than competing — but the shared vocabulary is a trap in RFPs and vendor meetings, so pin down which layer is being discussed.

7. What Media IT teams should do now

  1. Separate roadmap from release. Ask each vendor, in writing, whether the product is MXL-native, bridges to MXL, or is merely "aligned with the principles." The January 2026 survey suggests those are very different things.
  2. Check the infrastructure prerequisites. Vendor commentary lists NUMA-aware memory allocation, RDMA-capable networking, reliable PTP distribution and a security model designed in from the start. Budget for them.
  3. Decide who owns orchestration. The specification does not define it, so someone has to.
  4. Keep ST 2110 and SRT/RIST in the plan. MXL sits alongside them; it does not replace them.
  5. Benchmark it yourself. Published latency comparisons come from specific lab conditions. Measure on your own workloads and hardware.

Bottom line

MXL winning at IBC2026 is a strong signal that the industry wants an open, vendor-neutral way for software media functions to talk to each other. It is not yet a signal that you can buy a mature, multi-vendor MXL facility today. The sensible position for most teams is to track it closely, test it in a lab, and make sure every software-defined facility decision made now leaves room for it.
