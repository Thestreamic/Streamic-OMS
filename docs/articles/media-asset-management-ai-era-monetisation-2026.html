Media Asset Management (MAM) in the AI Era: Smarter Content and Faster Monetisation in 2026

MAM is the operational intelligence layer of a modern broadcast organisation. AI-powered metadata generation, smart search, and tiered storage are transforming archives from cost centres into revenue engines.

The Streamic Editorial Team 2026-03-25 569 words · 5 min read Analysis

🔑 Key Engineering Brief

  • MAM is the intelligence layer, not the storage layer — searchability determines content value
  • AI metadata generation reduces manual cataloguing effort by 60-80% on standard content types
  • FAST channel economics depend entirely on searchable, correctly tagged archive content
  • Parallel migration — running old and new MAM simultaneously — is the only safe approach
  • Tiered storage (hot/warm/cold) is mandatory for cost management at broadcast archive scale

Introduction

The media asset management challenge in broadcast has always been about discoverability. Storage capacity is cheap. Finding the right content at the right quality, with the right metadata for rights clearance, at the moment an editor needs it — that is the hard problem. A major broadcaster with 40+ years of television production in archive storage has a content asset worth potentially hundreds of millions in licensing, FAST channel programming, and OTT repurposing — if that content can be found. If it cannot be found because metadata is absent or inconsistent, those assets generate no revenue and storage costs are pure overhead.

How AI Enhances MAM

Automated Metadata Generation. AI extracts structured metadata automatically: speech-to-text transcripts, scene descriptions, face recognition for known talent, object detection. Broadcasters report manual metadata effort reductions of 60-80% on standard content types when AI tagging is integrated into the ingest pipeline.

Natural Language Search. An editor searching for "a presenter talking about climate policy in front of the Houses of Parliament in daylight" finds relevant content without knowing the original logging terms. This capability alone delivers significant time savings in news and current affairs production workflows.

Real-World Limitations

Low-quality archive footage. AI tools trained on modern broadcast content degrade significantly on 1980s and 1990s archive: low resolution, VHS-quality transfers, analogue audio artefacts. Retroactively catalogued archive has lower metadata quality than current production.

Legacy system migration complexity. MAM platforms from Avid Interplay EVS IPDirector Grass Valley Stratus are deeply integrated with production, playout, and newsroom systems. Migration is an infrastructure project affecting every workflow touching content — not just a data migration exercise.

Tiered Storage Architecture

  • Hot tier (online disk) — current production and recently aired material. Sub-second retrieval. Highest cost per TB.
  • Warm tier (nearline disk or tape) — occasionally accessed content. Retrieval in minutes. Significant cost saving vs. hot tier at scale.
  • Cold tier (LTO tape or cloud archive) — long-term preservation, rarely accessed. Retrieval in hours. Lowest cost per TB.

Conclusion

MAM is not a technology purchase — it is a strategic infrastructure decision determining how efficiently a broadcast organisation monetises its content assets. In 2026, competitive advantage lies not in storage capacity, but in the intelligence of the metadata layer and the efficiency of the workflows that depend on it.
