✦ Part 2: The Veteran’s Lens

The Science of Grain and the Hybrid Future of AI

Debunking myths in restoration and why Quality Control remains the ultimate barrier against over-processing.

By The Streamic Editorial TeamMay 16, 20268 min read
Venkatakrishna A
Domain Expert & Senior Colorist
Real Image Media Solutions (Qube Cinema) · Chennai, India

Welcome back to the conclusion of our deep-dive with Mr. Venkatakrishna A. In Part 1, we established the foundational philosophy of preservation and the limits of modern AI cleanup. In Part 2, Venki brings his decades of expertise from Real Image to break down the technical mastery required to manage film grain versus digital noise, the non-negotiable nature of Quality Control, and his vision for the hybrid future of our industry.

08. Preserving the Master

How do you ensure that the restored material remains faithful to the original creative intent?

I ensure this by:

  • Referencing original prints or OCN - original cinema negative masters when available
  • Studying the cinematography style of the era
  • Avoiding over-cleaning or over-sharpening
  • Consulting with stakeholders (directors, archivists), who took the creative decision during the original presented content
09. Grain vs. Noise

What are the most challenging aspects of maintaining natural grain, texture, and detail while removing noise or damage?

Grain and noise have to be looked at different perspectives.

Film grain is the physical structure of light-sensitive silver halide crystals in film stock. When exposed and developed, these crystals create the textured pattern we see.

Key characteristics:

  • Organic & random, but consistent in character
  • Moves naturally from frame to frame (temporal variation)
  • Adds depth, texture, and perceived sharpness
  • Part of the original cinematography aesthetic
  • Varies by film stock (fine grain vs coarse grain)

Noise is an electronic or compression artefact introduced during capture, encoding, or processing in digital systems.

Key characteristics:

  • Unnatural and inconsistent
  • Often includes chroma noise (colour speckles)
  • Appears in low light / high ISO / compression-heavy footage
  • Can look blocky, smeared, or “crawling”
  • Degrades image quality and detail

In simple terms, noise is interference that shouldn’t be there.

The biggest challenges are:

  • Maintaining fine detail while removing defects
  • Removing grain incorrectly results in plastic, lifeless images
  • Leaving noise untreated results in dirty, degraded visuals
  • The real skill is separating the two

This is why high-end tools like DVO Clarity, DVO Grain in Filmworkz Phoenix are used carefully:

  • Grain management (preserve or rebuild)
  • Noise reduction (remove without damaging detail)
10. Precision Fixes

How do you approach issues like flicker, gate weave, colour breathing, or motion instability?

  • Flicker: temporal analysis + manual correction for inconsistent areas
  • Gate weave: stabilization with edge tracking while preserving framing
  • Colour breathing: shot-level grading and luminance balancing
  • Motion instability: selective stabilization to avoid unnatural motion.

Note: Sometimes over scanning the element gives us advantage to make use of the perforation marks.
Each issue is treated surgically (temporal), not globally.

11. The Ultimate Barrier

What quality control steps do you consider essential before delivering a restored master?

Before delivering a restored master, quality control is treated as a dedicated final phase focused on both technical accuracy and visual integrity.

The process begins with a full real-time playback on a calibrated monitor to evaluate overall consistency in colour, brightness, grain, and motion, followed by targeted frame-by-frame checks in previously restored or problem-heavy sections to catch residual defects or missed artefacts.

A key part of QC is identifying issues introduced during restoration itself, such as over-aggressive noise reduction, AI artefacts, or stabilization errors.

Equal attention is given to maintaining natural grain, texture, and detail, ensuring there is no over-smoothing or inconsistency across shots. Critical elements like faces, fine details, titles, and graphics are inspected closely to preserve sharpness and integrity.

The master is then reviewed across multiple displays to confirm it translates well in real-world viewing conditions, and audio-visual sync is validated where applicable.

Finally, a fresh-eye review, ideally by another reviewer is conducted to ensure the restoration is invisible, with no distracting artefacts.

Allowing the audience to experience the film as intended is not just about fixing defects, it’s about catching new ones.
QC is as critical as restoration itself.

12. Creative Consensus

How do you collaborate with archivists, producers, or directors during the restoration process?

Collaboration in film restoration involves closely working with archivists, producers, and directors to balance authenticity, technical quality, and creative intent.

Archivists provide historical context and reference materials to guide accuracy, while producers help define scope, timelines, and deliverables. Directors or cinematographers, when available, offer input on the visual style, such as colour, contrast, and grain.

Across all stakeholders, communication is continuous and visual. I rely heavily on before-and-after comparisons, annotated frames, and review sessions to make decisions transparent.

The goal is to create a shared understanding so that every technical choice supports the original vision, while respecting the historical and practical constraints of the material.

13. The Master's Touch

What skills or instincts do you believe separate a good restoration artist from a great one?

What separates a good restoration artist from a great one is the ability to balance technical precision with artistic restraint.

A great artist has a sharp eye for detail and can quickly identify what is genuinely a defect versus what is part of the original image—especially when it comes to grain, texture, and motion. They have strong visual instincts, knowing exactly how far to push a fix and, more importantly, when to stop to avoid over-processing.

Beyond technical skills, patience and consistency are critical, as restoration often involves meticulous, repetitive work without losing focus. A deep understanding of film characteristics, formats, and historical context also plays a big role in making informed decisions.

Equally important is good judgment—being able to prioritize what truly matters in a frame and maintain continuity across an entire project.

Ultimately, a great restoration artist makes their work invisible, preserving the soul of the original while removing only what distracts from it.

14. A Hybrid Horizon

How do you see the future of restoration evolving as AI tools become more advanced?

The future of film restoration will be shaped by increasingly powerful AI, but in a way that enhances human workflows rather than replace them.

AI will take on more of the heavy lifting—handling large-scale tasks like dust removal, scratch repair, stabilization, and even complex issues like deblurring or missing frame reconstruction with greater speed and accuracy. This will significantly reduce turnaround times and make high-quality restoration more accessible.

However, the role of the restoration artist will evolve into that of a creative supervisor and decision-maker. As AI becomes more capable, the key challenge will shift from “how to fix” to “how much to fix”.

Human judgment will remain essential in preserving original texture, avoiding over-processing, and maintaining the artistic intent of the film.

We’ll also see more intelligent tools that can differentiate between grain and noise, understand motion context, and adapt shot-by-shot, but they will still require guidance to avoid introducing artefacts or altering the look.

Ultimately, the future will be a hybrid workflow—AI as an assistant and not as the artist.
