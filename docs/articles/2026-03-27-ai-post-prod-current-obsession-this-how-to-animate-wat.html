Milano Cortina 2026: How the Olympics Built a “Go-Anywhere, Do-Anything” Broadcast System

The Milano Cortina 2026 Winter Olympics didn’t just break sporting records — they showed how live broadcasting is becoming more flexible, more cloud-powered, and far less tied to one physical location.

The Streamic Editorial Team March 29, 2026 780 words · 4 min read

The Milano Cortina 2026 Winter Olympics didn’t just deliver elite sport. It delivered a glimpse of what the next generation of live broadcasting looks like.

For the first time at this scale, the entire broadcast operation behaved like one giant, flexible, cloud-powered machine. Whether a camera was positioned high in the mountains of Cortina or inside the main broadcast center in Milan, the system connected everything with remarkable speed and precision — almost as if it were all happening in the same room.

That matters, because modern sports coverage is no longer just about capturing the action. It is about moving huge amounts of video, audio, metadata, replays, and highlights instantly across long distances without slowing the viewer experience.

1. The Scale: More Cameras, More Sound, More Coverage Than Ever

Olympic Broadcasting Services (OBS) captured the Games on a massive scale:

  • 6,500+ hours of live and recorded content
  • 810+ camera systems across all venues
  • 1,800 microphones picking up everything from ski edges to crowd roars

This meant viewers around the world saw every angle, every replay, and every moment — no matter where it happened.

2. The Innovation: AI Highlights and 3D Replays That Looked Like Magic

Milano Cortina 2026 was the first Winter Olympics to fully use AI to help create content.

What the AI did:

  • Automatically created highlights within seconds
  • Helped identify key moments in sports like figure skating
  • Powered 3D “Spacetime Slice” replays, where the camera freezes and spins around the athlete

These 3D replays separated athletes from the snow or ice in under 20 seconds, giving viewers a dramatic, almost cinematic look at jumps, spins, and tricks. It wasn’t just cool — it made replays faster and more accurate than ever.

3. Connectivity: Solving the “Mountain Distance Problem”

The biggest challenge was geography. The main broadcast center (IBC) was in Milan, but many events happened far away in the Alps, especially in Cortina. To make everything feel connected, OBS used:

Ultra-fast compression (JPEG-XS)
This allowed video to travel from the mountains to Milan with almost zero delay — less than a single frame.

5G Broadcast trials
Led by Rai and the EBU, these tests helped send signals from remote mountain areas where running fiber cables was difficult.

A powerful 100-gigabit fiber network
This backbone linked every venue to Milan, carrying hundreds of data and video feeds at once.

The result: directors in Milan could control cameras, switch feeds, and run replays happening hundreds of kilometers away — instantly.

4. Sustainability: The “Vanishing IBC” and Virtual OB Vans

Instead of building one huge, temporary broadcast center, OBS tried something new.

The “Vanishing IBC” model reduced the physical size of the IBC by about 40%, relying more on:

  • Permanent data centers
  • Cloud systems
  • Remote production tools

Virtual OB vans

Instead of sending big trucks full of equipment to every venue, many production tools ran in the cloud. This meant fewer vehicles, less power, and a much smaller environmental footprint. It was greener, cheaper, and more flexible — a model future Olympics will likely follow.

5. The Big Picture: A New Era of “Anywhere-to-Anywhere” Broadcasting

Milano Cortina 2026 proved that live sports no longer need to be tied to one location. Everything — cameras, replays, editing, commentary, distribution — can now happen from anywhere in the world.

What this means for the future:

  • Smaller on-site crews
  • Faster production
  • More creative camera angles
  • Lower environmental impact
  • More reliable global distribution

Summary

Milano Cortina 2026 showed that the future of live broadcasting is distributed, cloud-connected, and highly flexible. By combining large-scale capture, AI-assisted content creation, ultra-low-latency transport, and more sustainable infrastructure, the Olympics demonstrated how a modern remote production system can work at global scale.

Impact

The long-term impact is clear: future sports events will rely less on giant physical facilities and more on smart networks, software-driven workflows, and remote collaboration. Broadcasters that adopt these models can become faster, leaner, and more creative — while also reducing cost and environmental impact.
